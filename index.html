Understanding the Greeks
Delta shown on this page
How much an option's price moves for a $1 move in HOOD, and a rough proxy for the probability it finishes in-the-money. A 0.20 delta call has roughly a 20% chance of expiring ITM. Lower delta = further OTM = smaller premium but less assignment risk. This is the only Greek this dashboard computes, estimated from each contract's implied volatility via Black-Scholes (see the tooltip on each row for the exact numbers) — it is not the exchange-quoted delta.
Gamma
How fast delta itself changes as HOOD moves. Gamma is highest for at-the-money strikes close to expiration, which is why a short call that looks comfortably OTM can suddenly swing toward assignment risk in the final days before expiration.
Theta
How much an option's price decays per day, all else equal ("time decay"). As the option seller, theta decay is the mechanism that pays you: the call you sold loses value simply from time passing, which is why covered call writers often prefer shorter-dated, faster-decaying contracts.
Vega
How much an option's price changes for a 1-point move in implied volatility. HOOD tends to run high IV, which inflates the premium you collect — but it also means an IV spike (e.g. around earnings) can make your short call more expensive to buy back, while an IV crush after the event benefits you as the seller.
Rho
How much an option's price changes for a 1% move in interest rates. Generally the least impactful Greek for shorter-dated equity options like the ones in this table; it matters more for long-dated (LEAPS) positions.

Delta is estimated from each contract's implied volatility using the Black-Scholes formula — it is not the exchange-quoted delta and can diverge from it, especially for wide bid/ask spreads or low-liquidity contracts. Annualized yield assumes the premium repeats every period at the same rate, which options premiums do not actually do. Always verify prices and Greeks in your broker before placing a trade.
